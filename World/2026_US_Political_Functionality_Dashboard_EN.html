What is being tested?

This review asks whether the United States still retains the minimum political feedback loop required to govern a complex state: observe reality → recognize the problem → identify causes → revise policy → align agencies → protect survival conditions → accept consequences. The finding is not merely that some policies are costly. It is that observable losses are increasingly failing to trigger policy-state changes.

1. Observation failure

Policy narratives are not consistently revised when intelligence, household conditions, energy flows, or market signals contradict them.

2. Exit-path blockage

Lower-cost loss-stopping options are politically rejected while higher-cost coercive measures remain active.

3. Policy self-cancellation

Military, sanctions, inflation control, Treasury financing, agriculture, logistics, and alliance management increasingly work against one another.

Core finding
The problem is not that policy can be wrong.

The problem is that policy stops changing after reality returns “wrong.” When that happens across energy, finance, diplomacy, and domestic logistics at the same time, a political mistake becomes a systems problem.

Illustrative correction failure: Bessent vs. Reich

Robert Reich’s case

The K-shaped economy remains visible through real purchasing power, essential-cost pressure, wealth effects, credit dependence, lower-income spending strain, and reduced labor-market mobility.

Scott Bessent’s response

The public rebuttal emphasized faster wage gains for lower-income workers and nominal spending, while also shifting substantial attention to personal criticism rather than addressing the full household-balance-sheet argument.

Physical Supply: Why “Money Can Fix It” Fails

Supply is end-to-end. A barrel, lubricant batch, or diesel stock that cannot reach the required user at the required time is economically equivalent to non-supply.

Crude→Refining→Group III base oil→Additives / blending→Storage→Pipeline / terminal→Tank truck→Farm / truck / plant / utility
Physical scarcity

Higher budgets do not create refinery capacity, suitable lubricants, storage, pipelines, trucks, drivers, or time.

Survival bidding

Firms, farms, logistics providers, states, and the military all have rational reasons to hoard or front-load procurement.

Distribution consumes the scarce input

Diesel must be used to move diesel; lubricants are required to keep transport and farm machinery operational.

Method, Confidence, and Publication Rules

Classification standard

Confirmed — directly observed in the source record.
Ongoing — observable process is active but not complete.
Strong inference — causal mechanism is well supported, but the final outcome is not yet directly observed.
Inference / not established — scenario-level reasoning only.

What this edition deliberately does not do

  • No invented monthly price series.
  • No arbitrary 0–100 political-functionality scores.
  • No pseudo-equation converting rescue spending into lubricant prices.
  • No claim that repo/FIMA support is identical to QE or direct deficit financing.
  • No claim that ICC sanctions have already caused a measurable, isolated UST selloff.
Counter-hypothesis

“The system is functioning; these are deliberate hard-power choices.”

For that hypothesis to hold, the policy system should display an identifiable final objective, success condition, exit condition, cost ceiling, domestic-loss tolerance, and coherent reason for accepting alliance and financing damage. The underlying report finds those elements insufficiently observable and therefore rejects the counter-hypothesis.
