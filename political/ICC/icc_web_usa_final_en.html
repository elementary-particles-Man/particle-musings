SECTION 1

Overall Assessment: A Collision Between Domestic Sanctions Authority and International Judicial Independence

This section examines the U.S. sanctions announced on 18 August 2026 against ICC President Tomoko Akane and Senior Trial Lawyer Abdoulaye Seye, separating four questions: U.S. domestic legal authority, ICC jurisdiction, effects on judicial independence, and practical spillovers to third countries. The United States possesses domestic sanctions authority under EO 14203 and IEEPA; the external pressure created by its exercise on an international court and on States Parties’ treaty performance requires a separate analysis.

🏛️ Overall Assessment: Tension Between Domestic Sanctions Powers and the International Judicial System

EO 14203 authorises the blocking of property and interests in property of designated persons when they are in the United States or in the possession or control of U.S. persons, and prohibits transactions by U.S. persons. The August 2026 designations of President Akane and others impose financial sanctions on individuals for conduct connected with an international judicial institution, creating direct tension with the ICC’s institutional commitment to judicial independence. The central analytical task is to distinguish the existence of U.S. domestic authority from the legitimacy and systemic effects of pressure on international adjudication.

【Core Issue in This Review】

EO 14203 protects not only U.S. personnel but also personnel of non-consenting U.S. “allies,” and expressly criticises ICC proceedings concerning Israeli leaders. The text of the order and the sequence of designations through 2026 therefore confirm that protecting allied personnel is an important purpose of the sanctions policy.

🔗 Strategic Implication: Financial Power Meets Judicial Institutions

Expansion of the sanctions increases diplomatic friction with ICC-supporting states, over-compliance by financial institutions, operational risks for international organisations, and perceptions of political risk in dollar-based payment infrastructure. These are geoeconomic costs that should be observed separately from the policy’s stated objectives.

SECTION 3

Legal Analysis: ARSIWA Countermeasure Doctrine, Jurisdiction, and Judicial Independence

The United States does not officially describe these sanctions as ARSIWA “countermeasures.” This section therefore separates three questions: how the measures would look if tested against state-responsibility countermeasure doctrine, the ICC’s territorial jurisdiction, and the independence of judges and prosecutors.

ARSIWA 42 / 49 / 51

Requirements if Countermeasure Doctrine Were Invoked

The United States does not characterise EO 14203 as an ARSIWA countermeasure. If that doctrine were invoked, injured-state status, a prior internationally wrongful act by the target, a purpose of inducing cessation, and proportionality would each require separate demonstration.

Rome Statute Art. 12(2)(a)

ICC Territorial Jurisdiction

The ICC’s position is that it may exercise territorial jurisdiction over crimes committed on the territory of a State Party even where the suspect is a national of a non-party state. The United States and Israel contest that application; this is the substantive jurisdictional dispute.

Rome Statute Arts. 40, 42, 48

Independence of Judicial and Prosecutorial Functions

Independence of judges and prosecutors is an institutional principle under the Rome Statute. Personal sanctions imposed by an external state for official judicial conduct create a distinct systemic concern: a potential chilling effect on judicial functions, regardless of the separate question of treaty obligations.

EO 14203 / IEEPA

Effect Under U.S. Domestic Law

Property and interests in property of designated persons that are in the United States or within the possession or control of U.S. persons are blocked, and U.S. persons are generally prohibited from transacting with them. Effects in third countries must be separated into direct U.S.-law application and practical spillovers caused by risk-avoidance decisions of financial institutions and other firms.

Counter-Hypothesis Review

Counter-Hypothesis to Be Tested:

“The United States is lawfully using financial sanctions to protect its sovereignty and nationals; the measures are neither interference with ICC judicial independence nor an infringement of third-state sovereignty, and the connection to Prime Minister Netanyahu is secondary or incidental.”

Question 1: Unlawfulness of ICC Conduct
【Requires Analysis】

The ICC relies on Article 12(2)(a) of the Rome Statute for territorial jurisdiction over Palestine. The United States and Israel contest its application to nationals of non-party states; that is the legal dispute.

Question 2: Concrete Harm to the United States
【Requires Analysis】

The designation rationale for President Akane and others does not identify direct harm to U.S. territory or property. The EO instead makes a legal finding of a “threat” to national security and foreign policy.

Question 3: Availability of Legal Objections
【Requires Analysis】

The United States contests ICC jurisdiction through political and diplomatic channels while also employing domestic sanctions. The distinct issue is the pressure those sanctions place on individual judicial officials and the resulting institutional side effects.

Assessment: The United States has a consistent policy position opposing ICC jurisdiction over nationals of non-party states. At the same time, EO 14203 extends protection to allied personnel and imposes blocking measures on individual judicial officials. That creates separate questions of proportionality, effects on judicial independence, and spillovers to third countries.
